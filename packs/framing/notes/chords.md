# Framing chords

A **chord** is the frame an introduction plays to justify its paper. It has a **root**, the move that justifies the paper, and two notes that voice it:

- **Literature note:** how the paper portrays prior work (scattered, cumulative, or contested) and how hard it pushes against it (completes, adds, or overturns).
- **Surprise note:** which belief the paper says it reverses, or none.

A root is **carried** when the introduction shows the trouble: it names the theory or belief at stake and says what it gets wrong, cannot explain, or wrongly limits. It is **gestured** when the pieces are there but the justification leans on a weak frame, most often that the topic is unstudied. Exact definitions are in `notes/codebook.md`.

Use only the names in this file. The figures below come from 1,000 published introductions (see `data/norms.yaml`). They say what published papers usually do, not what a journal requires.

**What the published record shows.** Two in three published introductions only gesture at their root, and 88% of those rest on the unstudied topic. Wider reach, gestured, is a third of all introductions on its own; theory repair, gestured, is another 18%. Puzzles are rarer (14% of roots) but are usually carried (two in three). Wider reach is the root most often left uncarried (6% carried). So the most common advice is to carry the root the paper already points to, and for wider reach that means saying why the theory was thought not to reach the new setting.

## Puzzles: something in the world is unexplained

### Standing anomaly
- **Move:** a known pattern still lacks a sound explanation. Either the field knows it is unexplained, or it credits a theory with explaining it when the theory's assumptions rarely hold where it is applied. The paper explains the pattern, and everything the old account explained.
- **Foil:** the received theory, which says the pattern shouldn't happen, or which is credited with explaining it.
- **Carried looks like:** the pattern, evidence that it is widespread, and the theory it defies, named. When a theory is credited with explaining it, the assumption that fails, named, with evidence or argument that it fails where the theory is used.
- **Fits when:** the pattern is famous in the field and the mechanism is general; for a failed assumption, conceptual papers, formal models, or data showing the assumption failing. 6% of roots; most common in conceptual papers and at ASQ, AMR, and OBHDP.
- **Fails when:** readers think it is already solved, disagree about what would solve it, or won't accept that they misread the theory.
- **Usual voicing:** cumulative or scattered, adds. Surprise: effectiveness, connection, direction, or one-or-many.

### Hidden anomaly
- **Move:** a pattern the field hasn't seen as a puzzle contradicts our best theories. The pattern is in plain sight and taken for granted, or the author's own data turned it up. The paper points it out and resolves it.
- **Foil:** the theory that, taken seriously, says the pattern shouldn't exist.
- **Carried looks like:** the pattern, documented, and the theory that forbids it, named. A pattern that is merely understudied is gestured. When the paper's own data turned the pattern up, the data enter early (not in the first paragraph), and the introduction says what the theory predicted and what the data showed instead.
- **Fits when:** the pattern is easy to document and the author can say why nobody noticed, or the design is qualitative, case, or abductive: 32% of qualitative and 15% of abductive papers, against 2–6% of the rest. 8% of roots; most common at ASQ (20%).
- **Fails when:** defenders of the theory don't accept it as puzzling, or a pattern from one case looks odd. It lands better when the resolution saves the old theory, and when the introduction shows that a pattern found in one case is general.
- **Usual voicing:** cumulative, adds. Surprise: value, compatibility, direction, or effectiveness.

## Challenges to an accepted account

### Rival account
- **Move:** the accepted theory explains the evidence, but so does a rival the paper proposes, ideally with fewer assumptions or new predictions.
- **Foil:** the accepted explanation.
- **Carried looks like:** the accepted explanation, the rival, and at least one prediction on which they differ.
- **Fits when:** formal or simulation models (3.6 times the overall rate), experiments, or data that show the rival's distinctive predictions. Most common at Management Science.
- **Fails when:** the rival predicts nothing the accepted theory doesn't, so it reads as a relabeling. Carried in only 42% of published uses.
- **Usual voicing:** cumulative or scattered, adds. Surprise: connection, effectiveness, or direction.

### Shaky foundation
- **Move:** a belief rests on thin or confounded evidence, and the paper supplies better evidence.
- **Foil:** the belief, and the alternative the old evidence couldn't rule out.
- **Carried looks like:** the belief, what was wrong with the evidence for it, and the alternative that evidence left open.
- **Fits when:** strong identification: natural experiments, experiments, new data. 20% of introductions at Management Science and 13% at SMJ, but 1% at ASQ and AMR and 5% at Organization Science.
- **Fails when:** others deny the evidence was lacking, or there is no lurking alternative. With no reversal (a third have none), it can read as obvious.
- **Usual voicing:** cumulative or contested, completes.

### Theory repair
- **Move:** the theory itself is flawed: it conflates distinct things, or doesn't imply what it claims. The paper fixes it.
- **Foil:** the theory as currently stated.
- **Carried looks like:** the flaw, named precisely (which two things are conflated, which implication doesn't follow), and why it matters for a prediction. "The theory has neglected a distinction" is gestured.
- **Fits when:** conceptual papers (44% of them), or data that pull the conflated pieces apart. The most common root at AMR (43%).
- **Fails when:** readers get defensive, or assume newer work has already fixed it. Carried in only 32% of published uses.
- **Usual voicing:** cumulative, adds. Surprise: one-or-many ("what seemed one thing is several").

### Wider reach
- **Move:** a favored theory explains more than we thought: a new level, domain, or outcome.
- **Foil:** the view that the theory's reach stops where it has been applied.
- **Carried looks like:** a reason the theory was thought not to apply here, or a reason the new setting is a real test of it.
- **Fits when:** an established theory and a new domain with good data. The most common root everywhere (36%).
- **Fails when:** it collapses into "nobody has studied X in setting Y", which is how 94% of published uses play it. Reviewers may also let it overclaim.
- **Usual voicing:** scattered or cumulative, adds. Surprise: usually none.

## An opposed pair

### Head-to-head
- **Move:** two rival explanations, and the paper's data or design picks the winner, or shows where each applies more strongly.
- **Foil:** two camps, each confident.
- **Carried looks like:** both explanations named, each with its prediction, and a design that can tell them apart. One belief tested with better evidence is shaky foundation, not head-to-head.
- **Fits when:** designs with discriminating predictions; most common in archival papers (11%) and at SMJ. It works best framed as where each mechanism is stronger.
- **Fails when:** one test can't settle it.
- **Usual voicing:** contested, adds. Surprise: direction.

### Dissolved debate
- **Move:** an apparent debate is misconceived, because both sides are special cases of a larger view.
- **Foil:** the debate as the field has framed it.
- **Carried looks like:** both sides, the condition under which each holds, and the larger view that contains them.
- **Fits when:** conceptual papers, or data showing each side's result under different conditions. Most common at AMR.
- **Fails when:** the debate is real, or the reconciliation amounts to "it depends."
- **Usual voicing:** contested, adds. Surprise: compatibility or one-or-many.

## Weak frames

These are what a gestured root rests on. Each has a fix that turns it into a root.

- **Unstudied topic** ("X hasn't been studied, or not in setting Y"). Acceptable only when the gap causes trouble for some theory. Fix: name the theory the gap troubles and what it would predict here. That makes it wider reach carried, a hidden anomaly, or a standing anomaly.
- **Process for its own sake** ("we know X affects Y, but not how"). Fix: show that the process changes a prediction, which makes it a rival account or theory repair.
- **Literature-only puzzle** (a tension that exists only between papers). Fix: find the real-world pattern behind it, which makes it a standing or hidden anomaly, or a head-to-head.
- **No stated claim** ("we explore"). Fix: state what the reader should believe afterward.

## Using the norms

`data/norms.yaml` gives, for each journal, design, and home literature, the share of published introductions playing each root, the share that carry their root, and what the rest rest on. Use it as a hunch:

- Say "published papers like this one rarely use this root", never "this journal does not accept this root."
- Treat a group with fewer than 50 papers as too thin to lean on.
- Design predicts the root more than journal does, and journal more than home literature, but all three are weak: each explains 3–7% of the variation. A paper that plays a rare root well is fine.
