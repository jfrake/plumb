Pack: framing

# Framing

## Issues
1. The introduction claims that a leader's explicit reaffirmation destabilizes the institution, but the findings show a market-level outcome (a larger speech-day rise in S&P 500 implied volatility), and the discussion restates the institution-level claim more strongly than the evidence allows.
   Check: F7. Tag: [paper]
   Paper's best case: the introduction itself says a destabilized Fed "creates uncertainty in the broader financial markets" (p. 3), so implied volatility is offered as the observable sign of destabilization.
   Evidence: p. 2 states the claim at the institution level. The outcome is ln(VIX_t/VIX_t-1) on the speech day (p. 18); a one standard deviation rise in the backing ratio goes with about a 1.0 percent rise in the expected range (p. 18; BR = 0.036, Model 2, Table 2, p. 56). Neither the institution's stability nor the market's taken-for-grantedness is measured (pp. 19-20 treat taken-for-grantedness by period only). The discussion says the Chair's reaffirmation "disrupts the institution" (p. 26) and then proposes that a high backing ratio "reflects low levels of cognitive legitimacy" (p. 34), which runs the causal order opposite to the introduction's claim. The construct gap between VIX and destabilization is raised separately by the editor reader; this issue concerns the claim the introduction and discussion make.
   Excerpt: "This line of reasoning suggests that explicitly reaffirming our taken-for-granted assumptions will destabilize the prevailing institution."
   Page: 2
   Severity: Weakens a claim
   What would settle it: the introduction states the contribution at the level the evidence carries (more backing in a Fed Chair's speech goes with a larger speech-day rise in implied volatility, as Suchman's warning predicts), presents institutional destabilization as the proposed mechanism rather than the finding, and the discussion's reading of the backing ratio as a measure of low cognitive legitimacy (p. 34) is reconciled with the causal claim or dropped.

2. The introduction justifies the study by saying we "still know little" about leaders who reaffirm assumptions directly, without saying what any theory gets wrong or why it was thought not to reach this case (wider reach, gestured, resting on unstudied topic; the record codes the root as shaky foundation, gestured).
   Check: F2. Tag: [judgment]
   Paper's best case: the introduction does name a theory (Suchman's warning, p. 2) and a belief it reverses (central bankers', p. 3), so the pieces of a carried frame are present.
   Evidence: the sentence that does the justifying is the unstudied-topic sentence on p. 2. Under shaky foundation, the introduction names no evidence behind the central bankers' belief and no flaw in it (p. 3). Under wider reach, it never says why Suchman's warning was thought not to apply to a guardian who intends to reinforce, or why the Fed is a hard test. The material for the second exists in the paper: maintenance-work research treats leaders' reinforcement as reproducing institutions (pp. 1-2), and signaling theory expects non-informational clarification to reduce uncertainty (pp. 27-28). As a hunch, about a third of published introductions carry their root (overall 33%, n = 1000; archival 34%, n = 375).
   Excerpt: "As a result, we still know little about the institutional consequences of leaders who try to reinforce the existing social order by simply reaffirming these assumptions directly."
   Page: 2
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: the introduction names Suchman (1995: 596) as the theory at stake and says why a Fed Chair deliberately reaffirming the framework to sophisticated, price-setting participants (pp. 3, 13, 22) is a case where that warning was expected not to hold, citing maintenance-work research and signaling theory as the accounts that expect reinforcement. If the authors keep the shaky-foundation framing, the introduction instead names the prior evidence behind the central bankers' belief and what it could not separate (for example, speeches treated as one unit rather than separating backing from claims and data, p. 14).

3. The theory that holds the view the paper argues against, signaling theory, appears only in the discussion; the introduction's foil is a practitioner belief.
   Check: F3. Tag: [paper]
   Paper's best case: the central bankers' belief is a real, consequential view, and setting a prediction against it gives the paper practical stakes (p. 3).
   Evidence: p. 3 names only central bankers' "prevailing wisdom", supported by speeches and one scholarly source (the editor reader reports that source could not be located; not re-checked here). On p. 27 the paper calls signaling theory "arguably the most dominant theory" of how communication moves markets and says the findings are "inconsistent with this line of reasoning"; pp. 27-28 then contrast backing with speech length. None of this appears in the introduction.
   Excerpt: "My primary finding also appears to be somewhat at odds with signaling theory (Akerlof, 1970; Spence, 1973), which is arguably the most dominant theory for understanding how communication influences financial markets (Tetlock, 2007; Graffin, Carpenter, and Boivie, 2011)."
   Page: 27
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: one or two sentences in the introduction that name signaling theory's expectation (that a leader's non-informational clarification reduces uncertainty, Blinder 2009 as cited on p. 28) as the scholarly form of the view the paper reverses.

4. The introduction promises an unconditional reversal, but the discussion recasts the finding as complementary to the intuition it reverses, under a condition that is introduced after the main results and never measured.
   Check: F5. Tag: [paper]
   Paper's best case: the main result has the promised sign (BR = 0.036, Model 2, Table 2, p. 56), and the crisis-period attenuation (p. 20) is offered as a natural extension of the same mechanism.
   Evidence: p. 2, "will actually disrupt, not reproduce, the prevailing institution"; p. 3, "not increase it". The high-taken-for-grantedness condition first appears as an assumption to be "relaxed" in a supplemental analysis described as "tentatively extend[ing] this story" (p. 19). The discussion then treats the crisis result as "additional validation" (p. 26). Taken-for-grantedness is not measured (argument_map.md, Scope conditions).
   Excerpt: "In this sense, my findings are not in opposition to this original intuition, but rather, complementary to it in that together they portray a more holistic story about the relationship between language and institutionalization."
   Page: 26
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: either the introduction states the scope condition up front (the reversal is claimed where the framework is highly taken for granted) and presents the crisis split as exploratory, or the discussion keeps the reversal as the introduction states it.

5. The discussion's wording outruns the design and the outcome measured.
   Check: F4. Tag: [paper]
   Paper's best case: the main coefficient survives several robustness checks and an omitted-variable bound (pp. 21-25).
   Evidence: the design is OLS on 339 speeches with year, topic, and audience fixed effects (Table 2, p. 56; p. 18); the outcome is one U.S. equity-index implied volatility measure (p. 18). The discussion says "proved" and "around the world" (p. 25) and presents "a simple yet potentially more general principle" with teacher and pilot illustrations (pp. 26-27). The introduction's own voicing (adds) is measured; this is drift in the discussion.
   Excerpt: "Using speeches made by the Chair of the United States Federal Reserve from 1998 to 2014, I proved that reaffirming the assumptions underlying the monetary policy framework causes uncertainty in financial markets around the world."
   Page: 25
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: the discussion states the result at the scope of the evidence (speech-day S&P 500 implied volatility, 1998-2014) and presents generality beyond the Fed as a proposition for future work.

6. The framing as a belief overturned by better evidence asks the design for something it does not supply.
   Check: F6. Tag: [judgment]
   Paper's best case: the paper does address endogeneity at length, with interviews on speech scheduling, a timing argument, and an ITCV bound (pp. 21-24).
   Evidence: a belief-on-weak-evidence frame fits designs that fix a named flaw in prior evidence. The introduction names no prior evidence (p. 3), and the main rival, that backing paragraphs carry policy news, is set aside by argument ("likely does not provide new information", p. 27) rather than tested (argument_map.md, Alternative 1). As a hunch, archival introductions play this root in 12% of cases (n = 375) and wider reach in 39%; rarity is not the issue here, fit is.
   Excerpt: "Indeed, it is well-documented that central bankers believe that reaffirming the assumptions underlying their monetary policy framework provides more transparency into the inner workings of their historically secretive institution (Yellen, 2013; Bernanke, 2015), and that this should reinforce the strength of their institution and reduce market uncertainty, not increase it (Plosser, 2012)."
   Page: 3
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: the reframing in issue 2 (Suchman's warning carried to a hard case), which the paper's evidence supports without requiring the design to overturn prior evidence.

## Checked and fine
- F1: the introduction's move can be named in one sentence and is consistent across its five paragraphs (pp. 1-3); there is no abstract to compare against.
- F3: the maintenance-work foil is stated as that work holds it, with a hedge ("they appear to do so without explicitly engaging its underlying assumptions", p. 2), and the discussion leaves those findings standing (p. 34).
- F4: the introduction's own portrayal (a cumulative line of micro-foundations work) and push (adds) fit the evidence (pp. 1-3).
- F5: the main result has the sign the introduction promises (BR = 0.036, Model 2, Table 2, p. 56; p. 18).
