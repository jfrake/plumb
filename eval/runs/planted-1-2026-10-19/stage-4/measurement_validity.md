# Measurement validity

Scope: the eight generated variables in `stage-0/fingerprint.md`. All are dictionary scores, a vendor index, or a readability formula. None is a main treatment or main outcome. The main regressor (backing ratio) is human-coded and the outcome (log change in VIX) is a market series, so neither is a generated variable here. Stage 1 computational flag 1 (no validation for the fear index) is built on below, not repeated. No flags were dropped (`stage-1/dropped_flags.md`: "None.").

## Issues
1. The business media fear index that carries H3 is averaged over a window that includes the speech day and the day after, so it likely records the news coverage of the same market move the outcome measures, not the prevailing emotional climate the theory names.
   Check: C8. Variable: Business media fear (moderator or mechanism, H3). Tag: [paper | judgment]
   Paper's best case: fear in business media is a slow-moving climate, so a three-day average around the speech approximates the mood in which traders read it (pp. 11-12, 16).
   Evidence: The construct is "the prevailing zeitgeist of the market" in which participants interpret the speech (p. 11). The measure averages daily fear-word shares "over a three-day window (t-1 to t+1)" (p. 16). The outcome window is t-1 to t0 (p. 13). On t0 and t+1, business news reports on that day's equity and volatility moves and on the speech itself. A rise in the VIX on t0 would itself generate fear vocabulary ("worrisome", "anxious", "panicky", the paper's own examples, pp. 15-16) in the same sources the index scrapes. That is an inference, not a verified fact about TRMI content. Table 1 shows fear correlated 0.10 with the outcome and -0.23 with BR (p. 54). The robustness check uses "a two- and four-day window" (p. 25) but does not say whether those windows also extend past t0. The timing concern for identification is raised in `stage-4/mechanism_discrimination_issues.md`; the point here is what the number measures: part of the score is reaction to the outcome, and part may be coverage of the speech itself.
   Excerpt: "I constructed a business media fear variable by taking the average of this fear index over a three-day window (t-1 to t+1) around the day of the speech."
   Page: 16
   Severity: Weakens a claim
   What would settle it: rebuild fear from days strictly before t0 (for example t-5 to t-1) and re-estimate Models 4-5; report the correlation between the daily fear value on t0 and t+1 and the same-day VIX change; if the vendor feed allows it, drop articles that mention the Federal Reserve or the VIX.

2. The fear index is a proprietary, backfilled vendor series whose method and sources the paper describes differently from the vendor, and no validation of it on this population is reported.
   Check: C1, C9. Variable: Business media fear (moderator or mechanism, H3). Tag: [external | paper]
   Paper's best case: Thomson Reuters MarketPsych is a commercial product built for exactly this purpose, scraping major business outlets (p. 15).
   Evidence: The paper says the algorithms scrape "business news media sources (e.g., Reuters, Wall Street Journal, Financial Times) using a dictionary approach" (p. 15) and gives no version, feed, or extraction date. The vendor launch release (25 June 2012) describes "human emotion in news and social media" scored with a "patent-pending 'meaning-generation' technology" (https://mondovisione.com/news/thomson-reuters-adds-psychological-analysis-to-machine-readable-news-thomson-r-2012625/). A June 2015 release describes "a pool of 40,000 primary global news sources and 7,000 social media sites" and "covering historical media from 1998 to the present" (https://financialit.net/news/thomson-reuters-expands-financial-markets-psychological-analysis-service). A June 3, 2015 report states "The Indices were launched in 2012" (https://www.mrweb.com/drno/news20969.htm). So values for 1998 to mid-2012, most of the sample, were scored after the fact (external fact). Inference: the source mix (blogs and social media barely existed in 1998) and possibly the scoring changed over the sample. Year fixed effects absorb level shifts but not a change in what a one-unit move in fear means, which is what the BR x fear coefficient depends on. Who produced the text (C9) is therefore unknown and probably not constant. Stage 1 found no validation statistic or cited validation (computational flag 1). Table 1 prints the SD as 0.00 (p. 54), so the scale of the moderator cannot be read from the paper.
   Excerpt: "Every five minutes, their algorithms scrape business news media sources (e.g., Reuters, Wall Street Journal, Financial Times) using a dictionary approach, reporting a daily measure of the amount of words appearing in these sources psychometrically related to fear (e.g., worrisome, concerning, anxious, fearful, panicky, etc.) in relation to all words in the business media discourse."
   Page: 15-16
   Severity: Weakens a claim
   What would settle it: name the TRMI feed (news, social, or combined), version, and extraction date; state whether pre-2012 values are backfilled and with what sources; report Models 4-5 on the news-only feed if the combined feed was used; report the H3 interaction separately for the pre-launch (1998 to mid-2012) and live periods, or with fear standardized within year; validate the daily fear score against human ratings of a random sample of business-news days drawn from the sample period.

3. The LIWC positive-emotion score that carries H2 was validated on personal and general writing, not policy speeches, and in Fed text its words largely describe economic conditions, so it may measure the optimism of the economic content rather than the speaker's emotional tone.
   Check: C8, C1. Variable: Speech positive tone (moderator or mechanism, H2). Tag: [external | judgment]
   Paper's best case: LIWC positive emotion is a published, validated dictionary that prior management work has used to measure tone in business communication (Pfarrer, Pollock, and Rindova 2010; Rhee and Fiss 2014; p. 15).
   Evidence: The paper's own examples of scored words include "good," "positive," "great," "favorable" (p. 15). In a Fed speech these words usually describe data or outlook ("favorable" conditions, "good" growth). The LIWC2007 manual lists its validation corpora as emotional and control writing, "113 highly technical articles in the journal Science," "714,000 internet web logs," "209 novels," and conversation transcripts, with an external test where "first year college students wrote about the experience of coming to college" (https://www.liwc.net/LIWC2007LanguageManual.pdf, via `stage-2/model_provenance.md`). Central-bank speeches are not among them. Loughran and McDonald (2011) show general-purpose dictionaries misclassify financial text, finding "almost three-fourths (73.8%) of the negative word counts" from the Harvard list misclassified in 10-Ks (https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf, via `stage-2/setting.md`). The paper uses Loughran-McDonald for speech uncertainty (p. 17) but a general dictionary for the tone moderator. The C1 exception (published validation on a comparable population) does not apply, because the validated population is not comparable. Inference: if positive tone tracks good economic news, the BR x tone interaction can arise without any emotional framing, which overlaps with the "crowded-out outlook information" and "policy news" alternatives kept at Stage 3 (`stage-3/alternatives.md`). Positive tone correlates -0.41 with word count and 0.22 with the unemployment rate (Table 1, p. 54).
   Excerpt: "Positive emotional content is captured using a dictionary approach, whereby the percentage of words psychometrically related to positive emotion (e.g., happy, good, nice, positive, great, favorable, etc.) are reported in relation to all words in a speech."
   Page: 15
   Severity: Weakens a claim
   What would settle it: report the words that contribute most to the positive-emotion score in this corpus; hand-code a random sample of speech sentences (drawn from the analysis sample, kept separate from any dictionary editing) for affective positivity versus favorable economic description, with intercoder reliability, and report the dictionary's precision against those codes; re-estimate Models 3 and 5 with a positive-tone measure purged of economic-condition words or with the Loughran-McDonald positive list.

4. Dictionary error may differ between the financial crisis period and other years, and across Chairs, yet the supplemental crisis analysis splits the sample on the two dictionary moderators.
   Check: C7. Variable: Speech positive tone and Business media fear (moderators); subsample definitions in the crisis analysis. Tag: [paper | judgment]
   Paper's best case: the crisis analysis is presented as tentative and "preliminary evidence" (p. 20), not a main test.
   Evidence: The 48-speech subsample is crisis-period speeches with tone "above the mean" and fear "below the mean" (p. 21), and the reversal (beta = -0.155, p = .022) is restated in the Discussion "as additional validation of this story" (p. 26). Inference: crisis-era news uses fear vocabulary (crisis, panic, collapse) at much higher rates, and crisis-era speeches use different economic vocabulary that LIWC may score as positive or negative emotion for non-affective reasons. Dictionary error rates are therefore unlikely to be the same in 2008-2012 as in 1998-2007 or 2013-2014. Chair writing style also differs (159 Greenspan, 166 Bernanke, 12 Yellen speeches, p. 12), and the sample-wide means used to split the crisis sample mix Chairs and eras. The paper reports no error rates for any subgroup and no Chair fixed effects (Table 2 note, p. 57).
   Excerpt: "I further split the sample of speeches given during the financial crisis to consider only those that contained high positive tone (i.e., above the mean) and low business media fear (i.e., below the mean)."
   Page: 21
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: define the high-tone and low-fear cutoffs within the crisis period (or within Chair); report the distribution of each moderator by period and Chair; if the validation in issue 3 is run, report precision separately for crisis and non-crisis speeches.

5. The LIWC version is not stated, and the "power" category the paper uses does not exist in the LIWC2007 dictionary it cites.
   Check: C8. Variable: Speech power (control); also Speech positive tone and Speech negative tone. Tag: [external | paper]
   Paper's best case: LIWC is a standard tool and the categories are documented in its manuals.
   Evidence: The paper cites LIWC2007 (Pennebaker, Booth, and Francis 2007; Pennebaker et al. 2007; p. 15). Per `stage-2/model_provenance.md`, a full-text scan of the LIWC2007 manual finds no power category, while the LIWC2015 manual lists "Power power superior, bully 518" among categories "either a) new to LIWC2015, or b) substantially different from their counterparts in previous versions" (https://mcrc.journalism.wisc.edu/files/2018/04/Manual_LIWC.pdf, a 2018 upload of the 2015 manual). LIWC2015 also revised the emotion dictionaries, so the version affects the H2 moderator. Inference: either LIWC2015 was run and cited as 2007, or power came from a non-default dictionary.
   Excerpt: "Based on work that shows leaders exude a drive for power than influences markets (Winter, 1987; Emrich et al., 2001), I also used the LIWC software to control for speech power."
   Page: 17
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: state the LIWC version and dictionary used for each category, and archive the speech-level LIWC outputs.

6. The four "Fedspeak" and uncertainty controls each come from a different domain (10-K filings, psychotherapy transcripts, classroom lectures, Navy training manuals), and vagueness uses an unspecified "partial" dictionary, so they may not capture the obfuscation the paper uses them to rule out.
   Check: C8, C1. Variable: Speech uncertainty, Speech abstractness, Speech vagueness, Speech complexity (controls). Tag: [external | paper]
   Paper's best case: each is a published instrument "compiled and validated" by its authors (p. 17), and together they follow Blinder's (2001) description of Fedspeak as complex, abstract, or vague language.
   Evidence: Per `stage-2/model_provenance.md`: Loughran-McDonald was built on "a large sample of 10-Ks during 1994 to 2008" (Crossref abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01625.x); Mergenthaler (1996) was developed "to identify key moments in transcripts from psychoanalytic sessions" (Europe PMC, PMID 8991317); Hiller, Fisher, and Kaess (1969) is "A Computer Investigation of Verbal Characteristics of Effective Classroom Lecturing" (https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3102/00028312006004661); the Flesch-Kincaid formulas "were derived from test results of 531 Navy enlisted personnel" (https://stars.library.ucf.edu/istlibrary/56). The paper does not say which part of the Hiller dictionary it used; if it is a subset the author selected, it is no longer the published measure. These are controls, so the consequence is limited to the defense that BR is not a proxy for Fedspeak (`stage-0/contribution_defense.md`, defense 13). Speech complexity correlates 0.42 with BR (Table 1, p. 54), the largest BR correlation among the controls, which makes what complexity measures matter for that defense.
   Excerpt: "I controlled for speech complexity by using the Flesch-Kincaid reading grade level (Kincaid et al., 1975); speech abstractness by using a word dictionary compiled and validated by Mergenthaler (1996); and speech vagueness by using a partial dictionary compiled and validated by Hiller and colleagues (1969)."
   Page: 17
   Severity: Minor
   What would settle it: say which Hiller et al. word lists were used; on a small random sample of the paper's own speeches, check that paragraphs scored high on vagueness and abstractness are ones coders judge as obfuscating; or narrow the claim that these controls rule out Fedspeak.

## Checked and fine
- C9, speech corpus: the sample is all Fed Chair speeches from January 1, 1998 to December 31, 2014, with dates taken from the Federal Reserve website and checked against FOIA Request No. G-2015-00191 (pp. 12-13). Selection into the speech corpus is not driven by the analyst; the 1998-2014 corpus predates LLM-assisted writing, so that concern does not arise.
- C1, speech positive tone (partial): the paper names the dictionary and its source and uses it unchanged, following prior management uses (p. 15). The gap is the population, addressed in issue 3.
- C8, speech complexity: Flesch-Kincaid is a deterministic formula with no word-list misclassification (p. 17); its domain origin is noted in issue 6 only as it bears on the Fedspeak defense.
- C8, business media fear: the paper varies the window (two- and four-day, p. 25), which is the right kind of check; it would answer issue 1 if the windows end before t0.
- C7, outcome and treatment: the outcome (log VIX change, p. 13) is not a generated variable, so differential classification error in the outcome does not arise from text measurement.
- C14: not applicable; no LLM output stands in for human respondents.
- Scope note: the backing ratio (main regressor) is human-coded, with paragraph-level Krippendorff's alpha of 0.88 on the first 60 speeches and .84 on the last 10 (p. 13). It is outside this agent's generated-variable list; Stage 1 recorded the missing paragraph counts and disagreement-resolution details (`stage-1/computational_numbers.md`).
