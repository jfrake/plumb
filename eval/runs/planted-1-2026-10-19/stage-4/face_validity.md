Paper's best version (contract 8): a speech that spends one standard deviation more of its paragraphs restating the Fed's mandate and tools is followed by a speech-day rise in the VIX of a size that matters for hedging costs, and this rise is larger when speech tone is negative and media fear is high (pp. 18-19; Table 2).

Already flagged upstream, not re-flagged here (numeric_fidelity.md Issues 1-16; arithmetic.md): the 1.0 / 3.5 / 2.5 percent arithmetic (Issues 6-8), the *** on BR in Model 2 (Issue 2), the placebo at p = .017 (Issue 1), ITCV 0.014 vs Figure 7 (Issue 5), N 337 vs 339 (Issue 9), the 48-speech subsample df (Issue 12), SD(fear) printed 0.00 (Issue 8), and the zero SE on word count (arithmetic.md). I cite these only where they bear on magnitude.

### Benchmark Comparisons

#### BR main effect: one SD more backing raises the VIX about 0.8 percent on the speech day
- **Benchmark 1 (external, fetched):** On scheduled FOMC meeting days, 1999-2012, "The mean raw (percentage) change in VIX level is -0.703 (-2.78%) and the median change is -0.540 (-2.57%)." The VIX "declines about 75% of the time on FOMC meeting days (83 out of 112)." The authors summarize prior work: "Nikkinen and Sahlstrom (2004), Chen and Clements (2007), Vahamaa and Aijo (2011) and Krieger et al. (2012) document VIX declines on FOMC (Federal Open Market Committee) meeting days ... and conclude that FOMC meetings are generally uncertainty resolving." (Krieger, Mauck and Vasquez, MPRA Paper 52959, 14 January 2014, https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/52959/1/MPRA_paper_52959.pdf). Vähämaa and Äijö (2011, J. Futures Markets 31(10)) also report that S&P 500 implied volatility tends to fall after FOMC meetings (abstract via https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v31y2011i10p995-1010.html; full text not fetched).
- **Benchmark 2 (external, from stage-2/setting.md and contribution.md, fetched there):** Kohn and Sack (2003, FEDS 2003-55) find for Greenspan's speeches "we do not find significant effects on any of the financial variables."
- **Benchmark 3 (paper):** Within the paper's own Table 2, BR's per-SD effect (0.8 percent) is smaller than speech abstraction (1.0 percent), dissent president (1.3 percent), and same-day testimony (2.7 percent for a dummy).
- **Assessment:** Plausible in size. Verified fact: a one-SD BR effect is about 0.13 SD of the speech-day DV and about 28 percent of the average FOMC-day VIX decline, in the opposite direction. Inference: the full 0-to-1 BR range (3.6 percent) is about the size of an average FOMC-day decline, which sits at the upper end of what a non-policy speech should move given Kohn and Sack's null. That is a reason to want the timing and placebo issues resolved, not a magnitude flag in itself. The effect is statistically fragile rather than implausibly large: it adds 0.008 to R-squared.
- **Paper's practical-significance translation (p. 18):** The paper turns the effect into "A $10,000 investment would hypothetically gross a one-day return of $100 (1 percent)" in VXX. Fact (external): Barclays' VXX prospectus supplement (filed April 19, 2012) gives VXX an inception date of 1/29/2009 and states "Your ETNs Are Not Linked to the VIX Index" and "These futures will not necessarily track the performance of the VIX Index" (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/312070/000119312512169921/d336960dfwp.htm). Inference: the instrument did not exist for speeches from 1998 to January 2009, which is about two thirds of the sample by Figure 2's bars (p. 50), and a 1 percent spot-VIX move does not translate one-for-one into a 1 percent VXX return. The hedging-cost argument (p. 18) does hold roughly: an at-the-money option premium is close to linear in implied volatility, so a 0.8 percent relative rise in implied vol raises the hedge premium by roughly that share. [external + judgment]

#### H2: positive tone reverses the BR effect
- **Benchmark:** I found no pre-cap study estimating how speaker tone conditions the market effect of central bank content, so there is no external magnitude to compare against. Internal benchmark: the moderation per LIWC point (0.056) exceeds the main effect (0.034), so the H1 direction holds only for speeches with tone below about mean + 0.6 SD. If tone is roughly symmetric, about a quarter of the speeches have a negative BR slope. The paper reports the reversal itself at +1 SD (p. 19).
- **Assessment:** Surprisingly large relative to the main effect.
- **Why it might be larger than expected (steelman):** In the paper's theory, emotions set the interpretive frame, so a sign change is a predicted possibility rather than an anomaly (pp. 9-11). That steelman holds for the sign. It does not explain why a 27-word difference in positive-emotion vocabulary should matter more than the backing content itself. Table 1 suggests what else positive tone tracks: it correlates -0.41 with word count, -0.27 with complexity, -0.28 with negative tone, and 0.24 with power (Table 1, column 3). Short, simple, upbeat speeches are the commencement and special-occasion genre. The laypersons audience correlates -0.34 with BR (p. 22). Topic and audience fixed effects absorb genre main effects, but not genre x BR. The moderation may therefore partly be BR x speech genre. [paper + judgment]

#### H3: media fear amplifies the BR effect
- **Benchmark (external):** Da, Engelberg and Gao (RFS 2015, 28(1)) build a daily fear index from household search ("We use daily Internet search volume from millions of households to reveal market-level sentiment"). The IDEAS abstract summary reports that over 2004-2011 the index coincides with temporary rises in volatility (https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v28y2015i1p1-32..html; verbatim volatility sentence not retrieved, full text blocked). This is a benchmark for direction only: fear measures move with the VIX on the same days.
- **Internal comparison:** the fear main effect is about -0.2 percent of VIX per SD and insignificant, while Table 1 shows r(fear, DV) = 0.10 (column 1, row 4). The interaction implies 0.046 of slope per SD of fear.
- **Assessment:** Not separately flagged. A moderator can be large with no main effect, and the clear mechanism for an upward bias, fear measured through t+1, is already on record (dgp_map.md, Critical Selection Point 2; mechanism_discrimination.md Alt. 4). The H3 magnitudes cannot be recomputed from the tables because SD(fear) prints as 0.00 (numeric_fidelity.md Issue 8).

#### Crisis subsample: BR lowers the VIX 3.4 percent per SD (N = 48)
- **Benchmark 1:** The average FOMC-day VIX decline is -2.78 percent (Krieger et al. 2014, above). The paper's estimate says a one-SD increase in a speech's backing share in this subsample lowers the VIX more than an average FOMC decision does.
- **Benchmark 2 (paper):** In absolute value it is 4.3 times the full-sample main effect.
- **Assessment:** Surprisingly large.
- **Steelman:** Daily VIX moves were larger in 2008-2012, so a fixed log effect is less extreme there than the full-sample DV SD implies, and the paper presents this as exploratory (p. 19, "tentatively"). Even so, the 95 percent interval (-0.155 +/- 1.96 x 0.063 = -0.28 to -0.03) is wide, the sample is cut on two moderators at their means, and the specification cannot be Model 2 as stated (numeric_fidelity.md Issue 12). The point estimate is better read as noisy than as a magnitude.

### Implied Aggregate Effects
- Mean BR is 0.23, so the backing content of the average speech accounts for 0.036 x 0.23 = 0.8 percent of VIX on the speech day. Summed over the 339 speeches, that is about 2.8 log points over 17 years if the effect were permanent. This does not imply aggregate volatility that should have been noticed, so there is no macro-implausibility.
- The aggregate check cuts the other way for the paper's framing. Verified fact: the paper measures one trading day only (p. 13) and claims it "proved that reaffirming the assumptions ... causes uncertainty in financial markets around the world" (p. 25), using only the S&P 500 VIX. Inference: an effect of 0.13 SD on one day in one market, with no evidence on persistence, is small for "destabilizing" an institution (pp. 2, 8-9). Whether the VIX rise persists past t0 or reverses is the evidence that would connect the magnitude to institutional instability. The paper does not report it. [paper + judgment]
- The mean DV on speech days is -0.0051 (Table 1). Speech days on average show a small VIX decline, which matches the direction of the FOMC-day literature.

### Cross-Specification Stability
- BR is stable across Models 2-5: 0.034 to 0.045, all within one SE of each other (Table 2). Adding the interactions moves the main effect little. That is expected if the moderators are centered.
- The interaction coefficients are stable between single and joint models: tone -0.056 to -0.049; fear 30.590 to 26.293 (Table 2).
- The robustness checks on pp. 24-25 report no coefficients, so their stability cannot be judged (numeric_fidelity.md Issue 15).
- The crisis interaction changes from -0.051 to -0.071 when year fixed effects are dropped (p. 20). Inference: part of the "crisis" contrast is between-year variation that year FE absorb. The paper's preferred specification has year FE, so the -0.051 (p = .088 one-tailed, about .18 two-tailed per numeric_fidelity.md Issue 13) is the relevant figure.

### Internal Consistency
- **Crisis heterogeneity does not add up (new).** The paper reads the crisis results as showing that backing "will not increase market uncertainty to the same extent" when taken-for-grantedness is weakened (p. 20). The split-sample point estimate within the crisis is 0.035 (SE 0.036), the same as the full-sample 0.036. The split therefore shows a less precise estimate, not a smaller one. The pooled interaction tells a different story. Crisis speeches make up about 30 percent of the sample (Figure 2 bars for 2008-2012: about 24, 22, 23, 17, 16 = about 102 of 339, p. 50). With a crisis interaction of -0.051, the pooled 0.036 implies a non-crisis slope near 0.05 and a crisis slope near 0.0. The split gives 0.035 for the crisis. The two specifications differ (the split frees the controls), so they need not agree exactly, but they disagree on the quantity the extension rests on. [paper]
- **Placebo vs main effect:** the pre-window coefficient (0.041) is 1.14 times the speech-day coefficient (0.036). On magnitude alone, the speech-day estimate is indistinguishable from the pre-speech association. This is a cross-reference to numeric_fidelity.md Issue 1, not a new flag.
- **Figures vs tables:** the Figure 3-6 slopes reproduce the Table 2 and text coefficients (numeric_fidelity.md). The predicted value at mean BR from Figure 3 (about -0.014 + 0.037 x 0.23 = -0.0055) matches the DV mean of -0.0051.
- **Short-run vs long-run:** only a two-day window is reported. There is no long-run estimate to compare.
- **Reduced form vs structural:** not applicable (no IV, no structural model).

### Construct Validity

**DV: market uncertainty = ln(VIX_t / VIX_t-1) (p. 18).**
1. Face validity: the theory's construct is heterogeneity of market participants' judgments, the "range of expected directions" (pp. 8-12). The VIX is the S&P 500's risk-neutral expected 30-day volatility. It measures how far prices are expected to move, priced under risk aversion. It does not measure how far traders disagree. setting.md documents the variance-swap definition (Carr and Wu 2004).
2. Content coverage: fact (external): Bekaert, Hoerova and Lo Duca decompose "the VIX into two components, a proxy for risk aversion and expected stock market volatility ('uncertainty')" and find "a lax monetary policy decreases both risk aversion and uncertainty, with the former effect being stronger" (NBER WP 16397, September 2010, revised July 2012, https://www.nber.org/papers/w16397.pdf). Inference: a speech-day VIX rise can reflect higher risk aversion with no change in expected volatility or in belief dispersion.
3. Discriminant validity: the VIX moves with equity risk premia, with S&P-specific news, and with Monday and holiday effects (setting.md). The "backing" concerns monetary-policy objectives and tools. Interest-rate implied volatility or the dispersion of rate expectations would be closer to the construct.
4. Proxy distance: moderate to high. Market price of equity variance stands in for dispersion of beliefs about a policy framework.
5. Known validation: the paper cites finance work using the VIX as "market uncertainty" (p. 13). Those citations validate the VIX as expected volatility. None validates it as heterogeneity of judgments.

**IV: backing ratio (p. 14).**
1. Face validity: the BR counts paragraphs restating "the nature and boundaries of monetary policy objectives and conventional tools" (p. 14). That corresponds reasonably well to "making the backing explicit." Whether those assumptions were taken for granted at the time is assumed rather than measured.
2. Content coverage: one paragraph-level share. It does not record whether the restatement is new or routine. The coded features for change and future orientation (p. 25) are reported without numbers. One of the paper's two illustrative backing paragraphs (Bernanke, October 18, 2011, p. 15) discusses whether the Fed has a formal inflation target, three months before the FOMC adopted one (setting.md: January 25, 2012). A backing paragraph can carry framework news (stage-3 alternatives.md No. 2).
3. Discriminant validity: in Table 1, BR correlates more with speech complexity (0.42) and speech power (0.37) than with the audience-sophistication index the paper offers as validation (0.35, p. 22). It also correlates with negative tone (0.27), unemployment (0.26), word count (0.23), and media fear (-0.23) (Table 1, column 2). Inference: BR also indexes a speech genre: long, technical, framework-focused speeches to expert audiences.
4. Proxy distance: low to moderate. The coding is human, with Krippendorff's alpha 0.88 and 0.84 (p. 13).
5. Known validation: the only validation offered is the audience correlation, which matches Bernanke's interview expectation (pp. 22-23). That is convergent evidence about what drives BR. It does not show that BR measures reaffirmation of taken-for-granted assumptions as opposed to technical content.

**Moderator: speech positive tone (LIWC, p. 15).** The general-dictionary misclassification problem in financial text is already on record (setting.md, Loughran and McDonald 2011). New here: the Table 1 correlations above suggest positive tone also proxies for short, simple, ceremonial speeches. That bears on the size of the H2 interaction. Proxy distance is moderate to high, and no validation is cited for Fed text.

**Moderator: business media fear (MarketPsych, pp. 15-16).** Algorithmic dictionary scoring of news. The paper cites no validation study (pp. 15-16). Units are unstated and the SD prints as 0.00 (Table 1). The window runs to t+1, after the outcome. Proxy distance is high. Retrospective scoring before 2012 is on record in setting.md.
