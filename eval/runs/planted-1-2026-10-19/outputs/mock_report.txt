Referee Report on Archival Test Paper 1

The paper asks what happens to the stability of an institution when a prominent leader explicitly reaffirms its taken-for-granted assumptions. It studies speeches by three Fed Chairs, codes the share of paragraphs that state the backing of monetary policy (the backing ratio, BR), and relates BR to the change in the VIX from the day before the speech to the speech day. The argument is that stating the backing lowers its taken-for-grantedness and so widens market judgments, with speech tone and business media fear as moderators. Mapping Toulmin's concept of backing onto taken-for-granted assumptions (p. 3) makes a hard concept measurable. The hand coding of more than three hundred speeches is substantial work, and the attention to drafting lags shows that you took reverse causality seriously. However, I have a number of comments and suggestions that I hope will improve the paper.

MAJOR COMMENTS

1. The pre-speech placebo is significant and at least as large as the speech-day effect, and the defenses against a slow-moving common cause do not close the gap. The p. 25 text says BR "did not predict" the VIX change in the two days before the speech, but the printed placebo is significant (beta = .041, p = .017) and at least as large as the speech-day estimate (0.036, Table 2 Model 2), with a larger t (2.4 against 2.0). Its controls are not described, so the comparison is not yet direct, but as printed the falsification test fails. A speech cannot move the VIX before it is given, so something correlated with BR can produce the speech-day coefficient without the speech. One candidate is contestation already under way, which fits your reading of BR on p. 34 as a sign of low cognitive legitimacy.

The endogeneity checks do not rule this out. Drafting one to six months ahead (pp. 21-23) excludes later shocks, not conditions that start earlier and persist. The 40 nearby pairs differ in BR by 0.21 (p. 23), less than the roughly 0.25 expected for random pairs if BR is near normal with SD 0.22 (Table 1). The prior-VIX controls (p. 24) have no printed estimates. The omitted-variable bound is 0.014 in the text but 0.030 in Figure 7, where several covariates exceed 0.014.

Please report the placebo with the Model 2 controls, estimate BR coefficients on daily VIX changes from t-5 to t+5, and test the leads jointly and against t0. Print the prior-VIX estimates, benchmark the pair test against random pairs, regress BR on lagged market and policy conditions, report one bound, and correct the p. 25 sentence.

2. The p. 25 control does not separate lost taken-for-grantedness from the reading that backing paragraphs carry news about the policy rules. Your theory holds that stating the backing lowers its taken-for-grantedness and so widens market judgments (pp. 8-9). A news reading predicts the same sign. The coding rule counts discussion of "the nature and boundaries of monetary policy objectives and conventional tools" as backing (p. 14), in a sample spanning the 2008 tool changes and the January 2012 adoption of a numerical inflation goal. Your October 2011 example (p. 15) predates that adoption by three months. If traders read such paragraphs as news, or as raising the odds of a rule change, ordinary asset pricing raises volatility (Pástor and Veronesi 2013). Your p. 25 control for paragraphs that discuss a potential change is reported without numbers and does not show whether restatement alone predicts the VIX.

Relatedly, meeting timing could produce the same pattern. Central banks, the Fed among them, communicate more in preparation for policy meetings and before rate changes, though little in the days just before a meeting (Ehrmann and Fratzscher 2007). You control for the last meeting's decision (p. 16) but not the distance to the next. I suggest reporting BR split into restatement and change-discussing shares, showing whether BR varies with days to the next FOMC meeting, and adding a pre-meeting indicator and its interaction with BR.

3. The outcome is the t-1 to t0 change in U.S. equity implied volatility, but the paper concludes that backing "causes uncertainty in financial markets around the world." What evidence beyond the speech-day VIX change shows that the policy framework, rather than near-term volatility expectations, became less taken for granted? The VIX includes risk aversion, which monetary policy moves (Bekaert, Hoerova and Lo Duca 2010), and it does not measure the dispersion of judgments named on p. 9. No non-U.S. market is studied. Please report BR coefficients for t+1, t+3, and t+5, add an outcome such as the dispersion of professional rate forecasts where data aligned to speech dates exist, and restate the p. 25 claim at the scope of the test.

4. The paper does not say what BR adds to finance research on Fed speech content, or to Harmon, Green and Goodnight (2015), whose model it tests. If the paper contributes to central-bank communication, you need to show what BR adds to known speech effects. If it contributes to rhetorical institutionalism, it is a test of Harmon et al.'s model and of the proposition in Suchman (1995), both of which you cite (p. 2). You cite Fed-VIX event studies (p. 13), but not the closest work. Bligh and Hess (2006) find that coded language in Greenspan's speeches, testimony, and FOMC statements forecasts financial market movements, a direct precedent for your result. Kohn and Sack (2003) find no average effect of Greenspan's speeches on financial variables. Born, Ehrmann and Fratzscher (2011) find that central bank financial-stability reports move stock returns and that speeches matter mainly in the 2007-10 crisis, which is relevant to H2 and the crisis split (pp. 19-21). Swanson (2006) finds rate forecasts grew less dispersed as transparency rose, and Morris and Shin (2002) give an earlier account in which more public information can lower welfare when agents also hold private information. Heaphy (2013), which you cite on p. 34, reports that explicitly invoking rules maintained role expectations, and Ashforth and Gibbs (1990) argue that attempts to increase legitimacy can decrease it. I would like you to state what BR adds to the Fed-speech findings, present the theory as a test of Harmon et al., and engage this work.

5. BR may capture missing outlook content and speech topic rather than reaffirmation, and the paper does not show that its coding was blind to outcomes. BR is backing paragraphs over all paragraphs (p. 14), and p. 22 calls speeches that "discuss the current outlook of the economy" low-BR speeches. If outlook content resolves uncertainty, this rival predicts the H1 sign from how BR is built. The topic dummies (p. 17) absorb differences between speech types, so the rival works within topic, and they may also absorb much of the contrast the theory is about. BR's raw correlation with the outcome (0.09) is below the 0.11 the Table 1 note gives for significance, so the estimate depends on the 58-df specification.

Relatedly, coders who knew which speeches moved markets could read policy discussion as backing, an error correlated with the outcome, and the paper does not say whether coders were blind. Reliability covers paragraphs in the first 60 and last 10 speeches only (p. 13). Please report the R-squared of BR on the other regressors and its within-topic variation, and build Model 2 up from BR alone. Code outlook paragraphs and add their share beside BR. Have blind coders re-code a random sample, and compare the BR coefficient on original and blind codes.

6. The standard errors ignore speeches that may share trading days, the sample was trimmed on residuals from an unnamed model, and most of the reported checks print no estimates. The paper reports 40 pairs of speeches within three days of each other (p. 23), so some may share event-window dates, yet the errors are robust only (p. 57), and the GEE check links each speech's error to the same Chair's prior speech (p. 25). Seven speeches were dropped for studentized residuals beyond plus or minus three from a model the paper does not name, which risks selecting on the outcome, and the remaining 337 (p. 12) do not match the 339 in Tables 1 and 2. The text says OLS (p. 18) and the Table 2 note says "robust regressions with robust standard errors". Apart from the placebo, the checks on pp. 24-25 report no BR coefficient, standard error, or N.

This matters because the estimate of 0.036 (0.018) has a one-tailed p of about .023, which supports two stars, not three (p. 56), and one SD of BR gives about 0.8 percent, not 1.0 (p. 18). Please cluster errors by trading date or week, name the trimming model and the estimator, reconcile the counts, and report the main models with the seven speeches retained. Add an appendix table of BR, and of the interactions where a check applies, under each check, and give the effect size with an interval.

7. The tone and fear moderators may not measure the emotions H2 and H3 name, and the conditional effects stated in the text are untested and do not match the estimates. LIWC positive words such as good and favorable (p. 15) usually describe the economy in Fed speeches, and general dictionaries misclassify financial text (Loughran and McDonald 2011). The existing controls do not separate tone from economic content. The text says that at +1 SD of tone backing "in fact, reduces" uncertainty (p. 19), but reports no test of that slope. If tone is centered, as Figure 4 suggests but the paper does not state, Model 3 gives a slope of about -0.023 (0.034 - 0.056 x 1.01), significant only under a strongly negative covariance between the two estimates, which is not reported. By the same arithmetic the 3.5 percent figure at -1 SD (p. 19) is about 1.9.

Fear averages t-1 to t+1 (p. 16), so it can report on the speech and that day's market move. It correlates -0.43 with unemployment (Table 1), so the BR x fear term may pick up prevailing conditions. The 2.5 percent amplification at +1 SD of fear (p. 19) is about 1.9 percent in total by your Figure 5.

Please score tone on non-backing paragraphs and with a finance dictionary, add BR x word count, rebuild fear from days before t0, and add BR x existing uncertainty to Model 5. Report conditional effects with standard errors, or narrow the claims to attenuation and amplification.

8. The crisis extension, called "additional validation" in the Discussion, comes from a subsample selected on both moderators and a model with more parameters than speeches. The Results call this analysis "preliminary evidence" (p. 20). The crisis-only estimate (0.035, p = .335, p. 20) is almost identical to the full-sample 0.036, which shows imprecision, not a smaller effect. Reading σ2 as a standard error, the interaction p-values of .088 and .026 (p. 20) match one-tailed tests, which the paper does not state; .088 would be about .18 two-tailed. The 48-speech reversal (p. 21) is cut at the means of both moderators, one measured after the outcome (comment 7), and comes from running "Model 2 again", which has 58 df in Table 2, on 48 speeches. If the moderators are centered, full-sample Model 5 already predicts a negative slope in that cell, and the non-crisis BR slope from the p. 20 model is not printed. Please report Models 2 and 5 for crisis and non-crisis speeches with N and tails, say which terms were kept for the 48 speeches, show other cutoffs, and estimate the same high-tone, low-fear cell outside the crisis. Otherwise, describe the crisis result in the Discussion as preliminary.

MINOR COMMENTS

9. The only source you cite for the step from lower taken-for-grantedness to more dispersed judgments, the 2008 Organization Science article listed under Thessaly in your references, could not be found, and its stated pages overlap another article in that volume (p. 9). The only scholarly source you cite for central bankers' beliefs, the 2011 Journal of Monetary Economics article listed under Brandvold and Halvorsen in your references, could not be found, and the pages it gives belong to other articles (p. 3). Page 7 says the backing is only more taken for granted than the other components, but p. 8 says it "typically goes without saying"; please say how backing the Chair discusses regularly can still lose taken-for-grantedness. The word-count coefficient prints as -0.000 (0.000) in every model, so the Discussion's claim that longer speeches reduce uncertainty cannot be checked (p. 28); report it per 1,000 words. Which recommendation, wrapping backing in positive language or discussing the assumptions more regularly, does the evidence support, and what result would show that the extra uncertainty deters risk-taking (pp. 30, 32)? Business media fear in Model 4 is 0.005** with a standard error of 2.924, which cannot be significant (p. 56). The Model 5 adjusted R-squared of 0.261 does not follow from its R-squared of 0.355 with 339 observations and 60 df, which give 0.216 (p. 57). The text-only estimates label their uncertainty σ2, which reads as a variance, not a standard error (p. 20). The stars on the positive-tone main effect require one-tailed tests, but the Table 2 note says controls are tested two-tailed (p. 57). Audience fixed effects remove five category means, not attention differences within a category (p. 57); a t0 newswire count per speech and its interaction with BR would address this. Under your mechanism, should traders who follow Fed policy closely react more or less to backing than others, and can you show which group moves the VIX on high-BR days (p. 22)? Year fixed effects nearly absorb Chair identity, but no BR x Chair term tests whether markets read backing differently across three Chairs with very different speech counts (p. 12). The paper describes the VIX as traders' estimate of the direction of the S&P 500, but it measures the expected size of moves, so the argument about optimistic and pessimistic directions is not tested by it (p. 13). The introduction says we still know little about leaders who reaffirm assumptions (p. 2) and uses a practitioner belief as its foil, while signaling theory, which holds the view the paper argues against, appears only in the Discussion (p. 27), where the promised reversal becomes complementary under a condition never measured (p. 26). The three language controls for Fedspeak come from dictionaries validated in other domains, and the partial vagueness dictionary is not specified (p. 17). Both interactions use a single linear product term, with no binned check that the BR slope changes linearly with tone or fear (p. 18). H2 and H3 extend Pfarrer, Pollock and Rindova (2010), as the paper says, so their space might go to the H1 mechanism (p. 28). Research on ease of retrieval in persuasion offers a tested micro-mechanism for H1 and a moderator, audience elaboration, that the paper could use or rule out (p. 10). The VXX translation does not follow from a spot-VIX coefficient, because the VXX began trading in 2009 and its issuer states it will not necessarily track the VIX (p. 18). The paper does not say how speeches on weekends or late in the trading day were mapped to the t-1 to t0 window (p. 13). Because BR varies by audience (p. 22) and some events, such as an awards dinner, likely fall after the close, any dating error may be related to BR; please state the rule and delivery times, and re-estimate without such speeches. The audience correlations show that BR varies with audience, not that it measures reaffirmation rather than technical content, and the benchmark is the expectation of the author of about half the speeches (pp. 22-23); please check BR against an independent coding of restatement. Table 1 prints an SD of 0.64 for the Washington, D.C. dummy, impossible for a 0/1 variable with mean 0.29, and prints fear's SD as 0.00 (p. 54); report it unrounded. The reference list misspells McDonald as McDonaold in Loughran and McDonald (2011) (p. 44).
