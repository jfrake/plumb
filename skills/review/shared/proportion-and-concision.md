# Proportion and concision

A review makes a few points well. These limits keep each point proportionate to its evidence and keep the review readable.

**Comment count.**
The profile's `comments_min` to `comments_max` comments, and the maximum is a ceiling rather than a target. Approach it only when every one of those comments is substantive — a concern about theory, contribution, design, or interpretation that could change how an editor reads the paper. Fewer substantive comments is a better review than more padded ones.

Minor issues do not each earn a number. Collect them into a single closing comment, one clause or one sentence each. How many minors reach the review is the user's call at the minors check-in, not a count to meet: list every candidate in `held_minors.md`, and carry every minor the user keeps into the draft. If a minor point cannot survive being compressed to one clause, it was not worth raising.

Agents should surface everything they find — self-censoring at the agent level loses material the synthesis needs for prioritization. The cap is enforced when you synthesize: prune, merge, and prioritize down to the target count.

**Comment length and evidentiary density.**
A careful human reviewer reads a paper once or twice, marks a handful of places, and cites what is needed to make each point land and no more. Write that way. Drafts that run 3,000-3,900 words with 15+ quoted phrases, 30-47 table and figure references, and 25+ decimal coefficients are arguing with the paper's footnotes rather than with its argument.

Hold to these limits:

- **Cite to establish, not to prove.** One number, one table, or one quoted phrase is enough to anchor a critique. When you have four pieces of evidence for one point, pick the single strongest and drop the rest — the other three are for the QC layer to check the point against, not for the authors to read.
- **Roughly 8-12 specific manuscript references across the whole review**, counting table and figure callouts, page cites, and quoted phrases together. A methods-heavy paper can justify more; a theory paper should use far fewer.
- **Report statistics the way a reader thinks, not the way a table prints.** "The interaction is not distinguishable from zero under three of the four inferential conventions you run" beats reciting four p-values to three decimals. Give the exact figure only when the exact figure is the point.
- **Do not parse minutiae.** Internal inconsistencies between tables, truncated figure titles, unreferenced appendices, and star-convention mismatches are minor-comment material at most, and usually not worth raising at all. They are never the substance of a numbered comment.
- **Zoom out before you close a comment.** After establishing the specific problem, name the overarching issue it belongs to. A comment that ends inside the detail it started in is a comment that never told the authors why it mattered.

The test: would a senior scholar writing this review on a Sunday afternoon, with the paper marked up beside them, have bothered to look this up? If not, cut it.

**Theory and methods balance.**
A review must not lean on methods and empirics — identification, inference, sample construction, specification — at the expense of theory, contribution, and positioning. Where a paper's central problem is genuinely empirical, the review should say so plainly. But methodological problems are concrete and easy to evidence, so synthesis reaches for them first by default, and a review assembled that way reads like a referee report from a different field than the one the paper belongs to.

When you synthesize, check the balance explicitly. Ask what the review's first three comments are about. If all three are methodological and the paper makes a theoretical claim, you have almost certainly under-weighted the theory, contribution, and construct-validity checks relative to the methods checks. The questions that should be competing for the top of the review are whether the theoretical argument is sound, whether the contribution is real and novel, whether the constructs mean what the paper says they mean, and whether the conclusions follow from the evidence — with empirical execution as one input to the last of those, not as the review's organizing frame.

This is not a quota, and a fatally flawed research design still leads the review. It is a check against reaching for the methods critique because it is the easiest one to document.
